# Write High-Signal Outreach

Write for a busy, informed, skeptical reader. Assume the recipient knows their company, paper, product, project, and terminology. Use only the context needed to locate the exact part the sender wants to discuss, join, or advance.

## Think Before Drafting

Do not draft from a company name, role title, and resume alone. Complete this reasoning first:

1. **Research the target**
   Verify the recipient's current role, the current project or hiring need, and the source date. Read the role, paper, product, or company material needed to understand the work.
2. **Read the demand side**
   Infer what output, bottleneck, or collaboration cost matters to this recipient. Separate source-backed facts from inference.
3. **Choose the sender's value**
   Name one thing the sender could build, test, own, improve, or help decide. Choose one proof that supports that contribution.
4. **Form the argument**
   Answer three questions in order. Why this recipient? What can the sender contribute or achieve here? Why should these two people work together?
5. **Choose the ask**
   Ask for the smallest next step appropriate to the relationship.
6. **Draft and cut**
   Write only after the argument is clear, then remove sentences that do not carry it.

Perform this work without making the user manage each step. Surface a short decision brief before drafting only when the thesis, recipient, or evidence remains uncertain, or when the user asks to discuss first. If the user asks for final copy, return the best supported version and flag only uncertainty that affects sendability.

## Require Sentence Utility

Keep a sentence only when it does one of these jobs:

1. Name the exact project, problem, role, or contribution the sender wants.
2. State what the sender wants to test, build, change, or discuss.
3. Provide evidence that the sender can do that work.
4. Establish a constraint the recipient needs to know.
5. Make one answerable request.

Delete sentences that only express admiration, enthusiasm, alignment, or a broad description of the recipient's work.

## Respect Recipient Knowledge

Do not explain terminology created or used by the recipient unless another audience needs the definition.

Do not write:

> Tool-R0 asks how agents can generate the experience that drives their own improvement.

Write:

> I want to work with you on Tool-R0, specifically to test whether generated tasks can be grounded in an executable environment and Solver success evaluated through task outcomes alongside tool calls.

The second version names the project, contribution, and question. It does not teach the author their own work.

## Build One Argument

Build around these three decisions:

1. **Why this target**
   Locate the exact role, project, system, or open question. Do not praise or summarize it.
2. **What the sender can contribute or achieve**
   Name a deliverable, responsibility, experiment, decision, or research question.
3. **Why the collaboration fits**
   Give one proof with the same underlying problem or operating demand. Add execution proof only when supervision cost matters.

End with one question the recipient can answer.

Do not turn the message into a biography. New context must compete for space. Replace a weaker proof when a stronger one appears.

## Select Proof

Prefer one piece of evidence over a skill list. Name what the sender personally built, changed, tested, shipped, or learned and the constraint that makes it relevant. Add a link when it verifies the claim.

For research outreach, connect prior work through a shared technical structure such as policy interaction, reward design, evaluation, or environment design. Do not claim relevance merely because both projects involve AI.

For company outreach, lead with what the sender can deliver. Use `I can build`, `I can own`, `I would bring`, or the concrete deliverable itself when supported. Do not lead with `I learned` unless the judgment learned is the evidence the role needs.

## Reject False Equivalence

Do not collapse resemblance into identity. Remove claims such as:

- I built the same thing you did
- I hand-rolled what your company does
- I did this before your company existed
- This role is basically my current job
- My playbook maps straight onto your problem

These claims erase differences, provoke disagreement, and usually exceed the evidence. Replace them with a bounded comparison:

```text
I can own [specific output]. At [project], I built [relevant proof] under [relevant constraint].
```

Do not use benchmark numbers, internal percentages, customer claims, company histories, or equivalence claims without primary evidence. A precise unsupported claim is lower signal than a bounded verified one.

## Edit Language

- Use nouns and verbs before adjectives.
- Prefer facts over self-ratings.
- Keep the recipient's terminology when it is the shortest precise language.
- Remove throat-clearing openings.
- Do not call a proposal the recipient's `next stage` unless a source confirms it.
- Use `I want to explore whether`, `I would like to test`, or `Could we discuss whether` for the sender's hypothesis.
- Preserve technical distinctions. A final text answer, an executed task outcome, and a matched tool-call trajectory are not interchangeable.
- Preserve the user's latest approved wording. Do not restore a rejected framing during later revisions.
- Treat requests for no dashes, colons, adjectives, adverbs, or filler as constraints.

Remove these phrases unless the same sentence supplies evidence:

- I am passionate about
- I am deeply interested in
- Your work is fascinating
- Your innovative approach
- This aligns with my interests
- I care about the question of
- I was especially impressed by
- I believe I would be a great fit
- I have a strong personality for startups
- I would love the opportunity to
- I hope this email finds you well

Show interest through the specificity of the work proposed.

## Draft Research Outreach

- Read the relevant paper or project when sources are available.
- Separate stated limitations from the sender's proposed extension.
- Name one unresolved question without teaching the author their method.
- Give one proof with a methodological connection.
- State what the sender wants to achieve through the collaboration.
- Show reduced supervision cost through past behavior, not a claim of independence.
- Map one sentence to each question when the recipient requested a structured reply.
- Prefer question-first language because the contribution is an experiment, hypothesis, or research result.
- Use a CTA such as `Would you be open to a brief conversation about your current work and whether my background could be useful to an ongoing project this fall?` when the timing and claim are accurate.

## Draft Founder or Hiring Outreach

- Name the role or operating surface.
- Select one output the sender can deliver for the company.
- Use one matching proof.
- Prefer deliverable-first language. The reader is deciding whether the sender can create value, not whether the sender understood a lesson.
- Ask for a conversation, referral, or the right person, not all three.
- Do not invent an email address, employment fact, or product claim.
- Keep contact research separate from message writing.

## Draft Warm Referral Outreach

- Start in the existing relationship instead of simulating a cold pitch.
- State the target role or team and the timing.
- Ask the friend to route or refer, not to evaluate the whole candidacy.
- Add a forwardable blurb only when useful.
- Write the blurb in third person and keep claims verifiable.
- Keep the personal note, request, and forwardable blurb separate.

## Choose a Low-Friction CTA

Match the CTA to what the recipient can decide now:

- Researcher: ask whether the question and background could be useful to a current project.
- Founder or technical lead: ask for a short conversation about the named work or role.
- Hiring manager: ask whether the experience fits the role and whether a conversation makes sense.
- Warm contact: ask for a referral or routing to one named function.
- Artifact offer: offer a writeup, architecture note, or evaluation plan only when it exists or the sender is ready to produce it.

Avoid dropping `Would love 20 minutes` after a compressed claim. Give the recipient a reason for the conversation and make the decision they are being asked to make explicit.

## Return Paste-Ready Copy

Default to 60 to 110 words. Obey an exact sentence count when requested.

Return only:

```text
Subject: [specific subject]

[Greeting]

[Message]

[Signature]
```

Do not explain why the draft works unless asked. If one unresolved fact affects sendability, add one line after the draft labeled `Check before sending`.

## Run the Final Check

1. Delete facts the recipient already knows unless needed to locate the sender's point.
2. Label every sentence as intent, proposal, proof, constraint, or ask. Delete anything without a label.
3. Confirm every proof supports one proposed contribution.
4. Separate verified facts, sender experience, and proposed ideas.
5. Remove repeated nouns, setup phrases, adjective stacks, and explanations of the draft.
6. Ensure the recipient can answer the final question in one line.
7. Search for false equivalence: `same thing`, `before it existed`, `basically my job`, and `maps straight onto`.
8. Confirm the CTA names what the conversation or referral is for.

When revising an approved draft, change only the requested sentence unless the change creates a contradiction. When the user rejects a sentence as generic or distracting, remove its function instead of preserving it with synonyms.
